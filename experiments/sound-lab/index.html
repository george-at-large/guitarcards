Sound Lab Notebook

What would music sound like if it came from a mind and body that aren't human? Random noise is strange but isn't music, and familiar music isn't strange. These sketches aim for the space in between: sound that follows rules you can learn, but rules no human tradition uses.

There are three kinds of clip here. Alien pieces test ideas for a made-up musical culture. Reference pieces borrow principles from real human traditions outside Western music, for comparison. Phase pieces use the slow drift-and-lock process Steve Reich made famous. Headphones help; several pieces use left and right as part of the music.

How these were made

Every sound was written as code. No recordings, samples, instrument libraries or AI audio generators were used. Claude, Anthropic's AI model, wrote and ran short Python programs that calculate each sound wave sample by sample, 44,100 numbers per second.

  • Instruments are built by adding up pure tones (each instrument's overtones), plus simple physical models of a piano, metal bars, drums and a gong.
  • Rooms come from a calculated reverb, not a real space.
  • Rules come from the science of hearing (a model of how rough two notes sound together) and from simulations of players who listen to each other.

Claude can't hear. It checked its work with pictures of the sound (shown under each player), measurements of roughness, and note counts. A person listens and steers. The code re-creates every clip exactly.

A · Alien and unusual

Each one tests a single step from the longer plan: an alien body, an alien ear, a culture that grows its own conventions.

A1

Body-derived scale

0:49

An invented instrument whose overtones don't line up like ours, and a scale worked out from that instrument alone. You hear the same tune twice: first on an ordinary instrument, where it should sound out of tune, then on its own instrument, where it should sound at rest.

What

I invented an instrument body whose overtones are stretched: partial n sits at 2.2log₂ n instead of n, so its "octave" is a ratio of 2.2 (1365 cents) rather than 2. I ran the Plomp–Levelt / Sethares roughness model over every interval and took the dips in the dissonance curve as the scale. No human interval was chosen; the scale fell out of the body.

Then I played the same melody, dyads and drone twice. The first half uses an ordinary harmonic timbre. After a short gap, the second half uses the stretched body the scale came from.

Why

This is the founding move of the plan. On a harmonic instrument the derived scale should sound sour, because its intervals don't line up with harmonic overtones. On its own body the same notes should lock and sound settled. If you hear that flip, consonance belongs to the body, and an alien culture would inherit a different sense of "in tune" from its instruments.

Listen for
  • The held chords at about 4, 10, 14 and 19 s (sour), then the same chords at about 28, 34, 38 and 43 s (smoother, bell-like).
  • Leaps by a "pseudo-octave": slightly too wide to be an octave, yet they sound like home on the second timbre.

A4

Coupled-oscillator rhythm

0:49

Twelve simulated drummers, each wanting a different tempo, are gradually made to listen to each other. Nobody wrote a rhythm. They fall into step, then split into two groups playing in each other's gaps.

What

Twelve percussion agents, each with its own preferred tempo between 105 and 147 bpm, simulated as Kuramoto oscillators. Each one strikes when its phase wraps around. Nobody writes a rhythm; I only change how strongly the agents listen to each other:

  • 0–6 s: no coupling, so free-running chaos.
  • 6–21 s: attraction ramps up and they lock to a shared pulse (synchrony 0.99).
  • 21–39 s: plain attraction turns into repulsion, while a second-harmonic term still rewards being exactly half a beat apart. Unison becomes unstable, so the group splits into two clusters (the seven slower agents and the five faster ones), each on the other's off-beat (synchrony 0.06, two-cluster order 0.9).
  • 39–45 s: coupling is withdrawn and the clusters begin to loosen as the sound fades.

The pitched voices use A1's alien scale, and the metal voices use A1's stretched body.

Why

In the full plan, rhythm should come from bodies entraining to each other, not from a grid we impose. This tests whether coupling alone produces recognisable rhythmic idioms. It does produce one, and it isn't trivial: a hocket. My first attempt simply locked into unison and stayed there. Working out the stability conditions (unison is unstable when K₁ + 2K₂ < 0) is what produced the split.

Listen for
  • The moment the clatter "catches" into one pulse, around 10–14 s.
  • The pulse cracking in two around 22–26 s and becoming an interlocking double-time pattern.
  • Wear headphones: agents are spread left to right in order of their natural tempo.
agents 12 natural tempi 105–147 bpm shared pulse 130 bpm sync r₁ 0.05 → 0.99 → 0.06

B · Existing styles, as reference points

Human traditions that already sit outside Western common practice. They mark where "unfamiliar but human" lies, so we can tell when an alien piece has really left it.

B1

Gamelan-inspired interlocking

0:45

Borrows ideas from Indonesian gamelan: a non-Western tuning, a melody that cycles under gong strokes, two parts that interlock into one fast line, and paired instruments tuned slightly apart so every note shimmers. It's an homage built from those principles, not a recreation.

What

This borrows gamelan principles; it isn't a recreation.

  • Tuning: a pelog-like unequal tuning, with a 5-note subset in use.
  • Cycle: a 16-beat gong cycle with fixed punctuation (gong at the end, kempul mid-cycle, kenong every 4th beat, kethuk-like off-beats).
  • Skeleton: a slow skeletal melody on paired metallophones.
  • Interlock: two interlocking parts, one hard left and one hard right. Each plays every other subdivision, and together they make one fast line that heads for the next skeletal note.
  • Shimmer: each pair is tuned about 6 Hz apart, so every unison beats (ombak).
  • Tempo: it surges from 60 to 105 bpm, then slows into the final gong.
Why

It's a strong human control. The tuning isn't 12-tone, the texture is interlocking and the form is cyclic, yet it's unmistakably human: it anticipates goals, marks cycles and accelerates toward climaxes. If an alien piece shares these traits, it hasn't escaped human music. It's also a sanity check of the toolkit on music whose rules are known.

What's not authentic

It mixes Javanese-style colotomic structure with Balinese-style interlocking and shimmer, a hybrid no real ensemble plays. The interlocking figures come from a simple rule rather than real idiomatic patterns. Real tunings differ from one ensemble to the next, and the bar and gong models are crude.

Listen for
  • On headphones, two parts that make sense only together.
  • The 6 Hz shimmer on every note, and the gong closing each cycle.
pelog-like 0 · 120 · 270 · 540 · 670 · 785 · 950¢ ombak 6 Hz tempo 60 → 105 → 42 bpm

C · Phase music

After Steve Reich's phasing technique, using original material. Each piece is longer because the process needs time to unfold.

C1

Two-piano phase

3:32

Two pianos repeat the same 12-note phrase. One creeps ahead a note at a time and locks in at each new position, so new melodies appear that neither piano is playing. After twelve steps they meet again in unison.

What

An original 12-note cell (A3 E4 D4 A4 C4 G4 E4 B4 D4 G4 C5 E4, in eighths at 138 bpm) on two modelled pianos, one left and one right. Piano 1 never changes. Piano 2 alternates: it drifts slightly faster for three repetitions, gaining exactly one note, then locks for three repetitions at the new offset. After twelve drifts it has lapped piano 1 and they finish in unison. Piano 2's note times come from an exact phase map, so every lock is sample-accurate, and the count confirms 972 notes against 960, one full lap.

The piano is a simple physical-ish model: stiff-string inharmonicity, two slightly detuned strings per note, felt-hammer strike position. Piano 2 sits 2 cents sharp, and both pianos have ±2 ms of human timing jitter.

Why

Reich's insight was that a single audible process can generate complexity nobody plays. Your ear finds "resulting patterns", melodies that exist only in the sum of the two parts. I built the cell to avoid internal repetition, so each of the 11 non-unison offsets yields a distinct composite. This is a useful contrast with A4: both get structure from a process, but here the process is fully designed.

Listen for
  • The drifts: a flam, then a ringing blur, then a new pattern that clicks into place.
  • Melodies you can't find in either part alone, especially in the high notes.
  • The halfway offset at about 1:37–1:44, and the return to unison at about 3:10.
cell 12 notes · 2.61 s per offset 3 drift + 3 locked cycles lap check 972 − 960 = 12
